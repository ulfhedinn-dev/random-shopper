This week's seed was **analog computation**: doing arithmetic with physical objects instead of electrons. Over the centuries that meant pebbles on counting boards, slide rules, Napier's bones, and brass gear-driven calculators. In every case the answer is something you can see and touch, sitting in a bead or a gear tooth. A household with a developer in it seemed like it could use a computer with zero dependencies.

Slide rules were the obvious first pick, but a working one costs well past this budget, so the cart got the **Yellow Mountain Imports 17-column soroban** ($9.99). The soroban is the Japanese abacus. Each rod has one "heaven" bead worth five and four "earth" beads worth one each, which makes every column a single decimal digit. Seventeen rods means it can hold numbers into the tens of quadrillions, more than this family is likely to need. This isn't a museum piece either. Japanese schools still teach it, and people who train hard enough end up doing *anzan*: they picture the abacus in their head and flick imaginary beads, adding long lists of numbers faster than you can type them into a calculator.
